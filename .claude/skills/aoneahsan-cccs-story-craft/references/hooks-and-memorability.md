# Hooks and memorability — the curiosity gap, SUCCESs, and nine hook formulas

## The curiosity gap (Loewenstein, 1994)

Curiosity is the discomfort of a gap between what a reader knows and what they want to know. A hook opens
that gap; the piece closes it. Two obligations follow: the gap must be real (the piece actually answers it),
and it must close (an unclosed gap is clickbait, and the reader remembers being cheated longer than they
remember the content). Open the gap in the first line, close it before the end, and never re-open it in the
last paragraph.

## SUCCESs (Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick*)

Ideas that stick are:

- **Simple** — one core idea, found by stripping until removing more would change the meaning.
- **Unexpected** — a pattern break: a fact that violates what the reader assumed.
- **Concrete** — sensory, specific, a thing you could point at. "Cuts first paint from 3.1 s to 0.8 s", not
  "improves performance".
- **Credible** — a real source, a testable claim, or the reader's own experience invoked.
- **Emotional** — one person, not a statistic about many; the reader's own stake.
- **Stories** — a small narrative the reader can simulate; simulation is rehearsal.

Use it as a review checklist on the finished draft: which of the six is missing? Concrete and Unexpected are
the two most often absent in machine prose.

## Nine hook formulas

Each with the test "could a reader scroll past this line without a flicker?"

1. **The specific number.** "Four of the last five bug reports came in after 11 pm." A round number is a
   claim; an odd one is a fact.
2. **The contrarian claim.** State the opposite of the received wisdom, then earn it. Only when true.
3. **The confession.** "We shipped the wrong default for eight months." Honesty is a pattern break.
4. **In medias res.** A specific instant, mid-action (`frameworks.md`).
5. **The question they already ask.** Written the way they type it into a search box — this doubles as an
   answer-first heading (`aeo-seo.md`).
6. **The "you" observation.** Something true about the reader's day that most writers would not notice.
7. **The tiny story.** Two sentences, one person, one moment.
8. **The before/after in one line.** "Last month: 40 tabs. This month: one panel."
9. **The naming of a thing.** Give the reader's unnamed problem a name; they will keep it.

Pick one. Two hooks stacked cancel each other.

## Retention devices

- **Open loops.** Promise a payoff for a later section, then pay it. One per piece.
- **Pattern breaks.** A one-word paragraph after three long ones. A question. A number.
- **Promise → payoff spacing.** Never more than ~300 words between a promise and its payoff.
- **Subheads that carry the argument.** A reader who reads only the H2s should still get the point.
- **The last line.** End on the emphatic word; never on a summary or a "despite challenges" formula.

## What kills memorability

Adjectives doing the work of nouns · three of everything · a summary paragraph that restates · a hook the
body does not honor · a metaphor mixed with a second metaphor · a claim the reader cannot test.
